\section{Problem Statement}

FitFolio is a mobile app in which users earn virtual currency from their physical activity and invest it in a simulated market that follows real-world prices. This section describes the problem FitFolio addresses, along with its inputs and outputs, stakeholders and environment.

\subsection{Problem}
Building good fitness and financial habits early pays off for decades, because
both compound over time. Regular exercise builds on itself and so does money
invested early. Poor habits compound in the same way. Yet many young adults,
particularly students and those starting their first jobs, struggle to begin
either one.
Learning how to deal with finances in an informed manner can be difficult and tedious. Advice on managing finances is scattered across many sources, which often contradict each other.
Learning by doing is also risky, since beginners' mistakes cost real money. Likewise with fitness, it can be daunting to get started. Many beginners lose motivation before they see results.
As a result, many people put off both until later, when poor habits are already established and harder to change. There is no approachable, low-risk way for beginners to build both fitness and financial habits while staying motivated.

\subsection{Inputs and Outputs}

This subsection characterizes the problem in terms of its high-level inputs and outputs.

\subsubsection{Inputs}
The system takes the following inputs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item{Activity data from the phone's health platform (e.g., steps counted, workouts).}
    \item{Manual user data input (e.g., age, height, weight).}
    \item{Real-world market data (e.g., current value of stocks).}
    \item{User decisions in the simulated market (e.g., buying or selling shares).}
    \item{Educational reference content (i.e., explanations of financial and fitness concepts).}
    \item{User responses to learning activities (e.g., answers to knowledge checks).}
    \item User choices about what to share on the social feed.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Outputs}
The system produces the following outputs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Virtual currency earned from activity.
    \item Shared social updates.
    \item{User market portfolio value (i.e., current value of owned shares).}
    \item{User market portfolio trends and milestones.}
    \item{User workout trends and milestones.}
    \item{Simulated prices of assets and scenarios (e.g., a replay of a past market downturn).}
    \item{Explanations of concepts where they appear (e.g., what an exchange-traded fund (ETF) is when viewing one).}
    \item{Feedback on user decisions (e.g., why a portfolio's value changed, how diversified it is).}
    \item{User learning progress (i.e., which concepts have been covered and understood).}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Environment}

\vspace{1em}
This subsection describes the hardware and software that users need to run FitFolio.
\subsubsection{Hardware Environment}

Users will need an Android or iOS smartphone that provides step count data and has sufficient storage and processing capabilities to install and run the application. Wearable device support may be considered in the future.

\subsubsection{Software Environment}

Users' devices must be running a supported version of Android or iOS. Users will also need to grant the permissions required for fitness tracking. The application requires users to create an account and have a stable Internet connection.

Within the application, all currency has no real-world monetary value. Users will not need to link a bank or brokerage account.

\section{Goals}
This section describes the high-level goals for users of FitFolio. Each goal is a key selling point of the product and includes a measure of its success.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Learning by doing:} Users learn core financial and fitness concepts through hands-on, low-risk practice, with each decision paired with feedback explaining what happened and why. \textit{Measured by:} a before-and-after knowledge check on a defined set of core concepts, where at least 70\% of usability test participants improve their score.
    \item \textbf{A human-centered, all-in-one experience:} Users see their fitness progress and financial learning together in one place, so they rarely need to leave the app to understand what they are looking at. \textit{Measured by:} usability testing, where at least 80\% of first-time participants complete the core tasks without outside help.
    \item \textbf{Motivation through friends, with privacy:} Users stay motivated by sharing their progress with friends, while keeping control of their data. \textit{Measured by:} users control who sees each shared statistic, and no raw health data is shared unless the user selects it.
    \item \textbf{Risk-free investing practice:} Users practice investing in a simulated market based on real-world market data, without risking real money. \textit{Measured by:} users can trade at least 50 real-world stocks at prices within 15 minutes of the real market, and can practice in market scenarios such as a replay of a past market downturn.
    \item \textbf{Rewards for everyday activity:} Users are rewarded for the activities they already do, as recorded by their phone's health platform. \textit{Measured by:} at least five activity types are supported.
\end{itemize}
\section{Stretch Goals}

The following are considered as potential extensions to FitFolio and
are not required for the successful completion of the core system:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Wearable Device Integration:} Support for fitness tracking beyond the use of a smartphone 
    by introducing support for wearable devices such as Apple Watch and other compatible
    fitness trackers. This can allow the application to collect additional activity data such as
    calories burned, workout duration and other metrics.
    \item \textbf{Social Challenges and Leaderboards:} Allow users to participate in fitness challenges with friends
    and other users and compare fitness or portfolio statistics through leaderboards
\end{itemize}

    \item What went well while writing this deliverable? 
    \\ Starting from a clear project brief from our supervisor helped us write the problem statement efficiently, since the core idea and its motivation were already well defined. This allowed us to spend most of our time on the decisions that made the project our own, such as naming it FitFolio and deciding which features to add beyond the brief. Dividing each document by section, with every member responsible for specific parts, let us work in parallel and made each person accountable for their contributions. Writing the Development Plan alongside the problem statement also helped, because it made us agree early on how we will work together, including our branching strategy, how issues are created and assigned, and how deadlines are tracked as milestones. Settling these processes at the start means less time spent coordinating later in the year.

    \item What pain points did you experience during this deliverable, and how
    did you resolve them?\\ \\

    \textbf{Davis:} The main pain point I had was writing the Expected Technology section. Our team members have technical experience in many different areas, so it was challenging to align on a single set of technologies. My first draft also only listed \emph{what} we would use, not \emph{why}, and the feedback I received asked for details such as how the app would communicate with the backend and which alternatives we had considered. I resolved this by checking in with the team early and often (e.g., in team meetings and on Teams), and by writing down, for each main choice, one alternative and why we did not pick it. Writing out the reasons made the trade-offs visible, which made them much easier to agree on. I learned that raising decisions early avoids conflict later, since a technology choice becomes much harder to change once other work depends on it.
    Another pain point was making the goals measurable before our initial design decisions were made. For example, how closely the simulated market can follow real prices depends on whether it uses real or generated price data, which we had not yet decided. What helped was our GitHub issue tracking: since each section of the document had its own issue, I could quickly see which design decisions were still open and coordinate them with the team. From there, I gave each goal a ``measured by'' clause with placeholder values that we can refine once those decisions are made, and reworded goals such as modularity around what users actually experience. This showed me that goals and early design decisions depend on each other, so they are best developed together rather than one after the other.

    \item How did you and your team adjust the scope of your goals to ensure
    they are suitable for a Capstone project (not overly ambitious but also of
    appropriate complexity for a senior design project)?
    \\ Our project began from a supervisor-proposed brief, so the core of the project was already defined: users earn virtual currency through fitness goals and invest it in a virtual portfolio that follows real market prices. Our scoping work focused on two questions: what to add to make the project appropriately complex, and what to limit so that it remains achievable. To increase complexity, we added two features beyond the original brief: simulated market scenarios, such as replays of past downturns, and a social feed that shares selected statistics without exposing raw health data. Both introduce meaningful engineering challenges, including a custom market engine and privacy-aware data sharing, which we felt were appropriate for a senior design project. To keep the project achievable, we limited automatic fitness tracking to data available from the phone's built-in health platform, treating additional devices as a future extension rather than a core requirement. We also committed to portfolio values that follow the market in near real time rather than strictly real time, since market data providers limit how often prices can be requested. These decisions allow us to deliver a complete core product first, while leaving room to expand if time allows.
