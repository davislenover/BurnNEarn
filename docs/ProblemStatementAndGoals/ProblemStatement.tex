\subsection{Problem}

\wss{What problem are you trying to solve?  Why is it important? Do not talk
about how you will solve the problem, only what the problem is.  AI is rarely
part of the problem.  The problem is ``what'' you want to do, not ``how'' you
want to do it.  The problem statement should be written in a way that is
understandable to a non-technical audience.}

Fitness and Finance are two correlated activities, that being they can compound overtime.
However, learning how to deal with finances in a informed manner can be difficult and tedious, there is so many pathways on can take
to manage their money. For example, investing in the stock market...but what makes a stock good to invest in? What are Puts and Calls? What is an ETF, what is a bond, so many questions yet
no single source for an answer. Likewise with fitness, it can be daunting to get started, so many different muscle groups, what is cardio, what is leg day, etc.
It's very important to be both fit and financially literate because much like how good financial and fit decisions can compound over time, bad decisions can too.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item What went well while writing this deliverable? 
    \\ Starting from a clear project brief from our supervisor helped us write the problem statement efficiently, since the core idea and its motivation were already well defined. This allowed us to spend most of our time on the decisions that made the project our own, such as naming it FitFolio and deciding which features to add beyond the brief. Dividing each document by section, with every member responsible for specific parts, let us work in parallel and made each person accountable for their contributions. Writing the Development Plan alongside the problem statement also helped, because it made us agree early on how we will work together, including our branching strategy, how issues are created and assigned, and how deadlines are tracked as milestones. Settling these processes at the start means less time spent coordinating later in the year.

    \textbf{Farid:} The first pain point I experienced was deciding who actually counts as a stakeholder. My first draft listed everyone connected to the project, including our development team, our supervisor, and the course TAs. I then realized this mixed up stakeholders of the course with stakeholders of the product. The course staff grade our work but have no stake in FitFolio itself, and since our TA is also our supervisor, the list was counting the same person twice. I also had to decide how to present organizations such as Apple and Google's health platforms, market data providers, and privacy regulators, which never use the app but still limit what FitFolio can do: the health platforms decide what fitness data we can access, and the data providers decide how closely our prices follow the real market.
    To resolve this, I focused the section on the product and grouped its stakeholders into primary, secondary, and external categories. This made the section clearer, and it will also help in later documents, since these external constraints will reappear as risks in the Hazard Analysis and as assumptions in the Requirements Document.
    
    My second pain point was formatting in \LaTeX{}. My stakeholder table initially appeared in the middle of a sentence and extended past the page margin. Each fix was simple once I understood the cause (a table placed directly after text is treated as part of that paragraph, and column widths must account for the spacing between columns), but I spent more time on formatting than on content. Next time, I will set up a working table template early and reuse it across documents, so that formatting does not take time away from writing.
    \\
    
    \textbf{Zaid:}
    \item How did you and your team adjust the scope of your goals to ensure
    they are suitable for a Capstone project (not overly ambitious but also of
    appropriate complexity for a senior design project)?
    \\ Our project began from a supervisor-proposed brief, so the core of the project was already defined: users earn virtual currency through fitness goals and invest it in a virtual portfolio that follows real market prices. Our scoping work focused on two questions: what to add to make the project appropriately complex, and what to limit so that it remains achievable. To increase complexity, we added two features beyond the original brief: simulated market scenarios, such as replays of past downturns, and a social feed that shares selected statistics without exposing raw health data. Both introduce meaningful engineering challenges, including a custom market engine and privacy-aware data sharing, which we felt were appropriate for a senior design project. To keep the project achievable, we limited automatic fitness tracking to data available from the phone's built-in health platform, treating additional devices as a future extension rather than a core requirement. We also committed to portfolio values that follow the market in near real time rather than strictly real time, since market data providers limit how often prices can be requested. These decisions allow us to deliver a complete core product first, while leaving room to expand if time allows.
