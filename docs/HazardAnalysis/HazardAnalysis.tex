\section{Introduction} \label{SecIntro}

% \wss{You can include your definition of what a hazard is here.}

\progname{} is a mobile application in which users earn virtual currency from
the physical activity their phone records, invest that currency in a simulated
market that follows real-world prices, learn the financial and fitness concepts
behind their decisions, and share chosen progress with friends. Although
\progname{} controls no physical hardware and never moves real money, it
handles health data, presents financial information that users may act on, and
rewards physical exertion. Failures in any of these areas can harm users, so a
systematic analysis of what can go wrong is needed before the software is
designed.

This document identifies the hazards of \progname{}, analyzes how each could
arise, and derives requirements that eliminate or reduce them. It uses the
following definitions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A \textbf{hazard} is a state or set of conditions of \progname{} that,
        together with a set of worst-case conditions in its environment, will lead to
        a loss~\cite{Leveson2011}. For example, a
        shared update that reveals a user's weight is a hazard: combined with a
        friend who sees it, it leads to a loss of privacy.
  \item A \textbf{loss} is harm to a stakeholder that the team wants to prevent.
        The losses considered in this document are described in
        Section~\ref{SecScopePurpose}.
  \item A \textbf{failure mode} is a way in which a component of \progname{} can
        fail to perform its intended function~\cite{IEC60812}. A component fails not
        only when its output is missing, but also when its output is wrong, late, or
        duplicated; this analysis considers all four.
  \item A \textbf{safety or security requirement} is a requirement that
        eliminates a hazard, reduces its likelihood, or reduces its effects.
\end{itemize}

The rest of this document is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{SecScopePurpose} states the scope and purpose of the analysis and
the losses it aims to prevent. Section~\ref{SecBoundaries} defines the system
boundary and divides \progname{} into components.
Section~\ref{SecAssumptions} lists the critical assumptions.
Section~\ref{SecFMEA} presents the Failure Mode and Effect Analysis (FMEA),
which is the core of this document. Section~\ref{SecSafetyReqs} collects the
resulting safety and security requirements, and Section~\ref{SecRoadmap}
states when each will be implemented.

\section{System Boundaries and Components} \label{SecBoundaries}

% \wss{Dividing the system into components will help you brainstorm the hazards.
% You shouldn't do a full design of the components, just get a feel for the major
% ones.  For projects that involve hardware, the components will typically include
% each individual piece of hardware.  If your software will have a database, or an
% important library, these are also potential components.}


The system analyzed in this document is the software the team builds,
configures, and deploys: the \progname{} mobile application, its backend
server and the application programming interface (API) through which the app
reaches it, its database, and the third-party libraries bundled with them.
Third-party libraries are inside the boundary because the team chooses them,
controls which version is used, and is responsible for how they are used.
Everything \progname{} depends on but does not control is outside the
boundary, including the phone's operating system (OS) and health platform, the
market data provider, the network, and the hosting service. Hazards that
originate outside the boundary are still analyzed when \progname{} must detect
or respond to them. For example, \progname{} cannot prevent the market data
provider from going offline, but it must not present old prices as current.


The hazards in scope are therefore those that arise in a component inside the
boundary, or in an external entity whose failure a component must detect or
withstand. They fall into five categories, which match the losses in
Section~\ref{SecScopePurpose} and the groups of requirements in
Section~\ref{SecSafetyReqs}:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Health and physical safety:} injury, overexertion, or harmful
        behaviour encouraged by rewards or fitness content.
  \item \textbf{Privacy and security:} exposure, loss of control, or misuse of
        personal and health data.
  \item \textbf{Data integrity:} incorrect balances, rewards, prices, or
        portfolio values.
  \item \textbf{Financial learning:} information that misleads users into a
        wrong understanding or a harmful real-world financial decision.
  \item \textbf{Availability:} outages or data loss that prevent users from
        using \progname{} or cost them their progress.
\end{enumerate}

Hazards outside the boundary that \progname{} can neither detect nor
influence, such as a defect in the phone's operating system or a breach of the
hosting provider's own facilities, are out of scope; they are excluded by the
critical assumptions in Section~\ref{SecAssumptions}.

Figure~\ref{FigBoundary} shows the boundary, the components inside it, and the
external entities it depends on. The components are described in
Table~\ref{TblComponents} and the external entities in
Table~\ref{TblExternal}. The components are a high-level division for
identifying hazards, not a final design; the software architecture will be
specified in the Design Document.

\section{Critical Assumptions} \label{SecAssumptions}

% \wss{These assumptions that are made about the software or system.  You should
%   minimize the number of assumptions that remove potential hazards.  For instance,
%   you could assume a part will never fail, but it is generally better to include
%   this potential failure mode.}


The assumptions below are kept to a minimum, because each assumption that
removes a hazard also removes it from the analysis. Each assumption states why
it is reasonable, which hazards it removes or limits, and what changes if it
turns out to be false.

\begin{itemize}
  \item[CA1:] \textbf{No real money.} \progname{} never transfers, holds, or
        displays real money, and never connects to a bank or brokerage account.
        \emph{Rationale:} this is a fixed scope decision in the Problem Statement.
        \emph{Effect:} hazards of direct monetary loss, such as unauthorized payments,
        are removed. Indirect financial harm, where a user makes a real-world decision
        based on misleading information from \progname{}, is still analyzed in
        Section~\ref{SecFMEA}. \emph{If false:} if in-app purchases or any other
        real-money feature is added, payment and fraud hazards must be added to this
        analysis.
  \item[CA2:] \textbf{Users are adults.} \progname{} is intended only for
        people aged 18 or older. \emph{Rationale:} it is designed for young adults,
        and collecting health data from minors can require the consent of a parent or
        guardian under the Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents
        Act (PIPEDA)~\cite{PIPEDA}. \emph{Effect:} hazards that apply only to
        children, such as age-appropriate content, are removed. The hazard that a
        minor gives a false age is \emph{not} removed and is analyzed in
        Section~\ref{SecFMEA}. \emph{If false:} if minors are later allowed to use
        \progname{}, consent, content, and social feed hazards for children must be
        added to this analysis.
  \item[CA3:] \textbf{Device integrity.} The phone's operating system is not
        rooted or jailbroken, and it enforces app sandboxing, secure storage, and its
        permission model as documented. \emph{Rationale:} the team cannot detect or
        defend against every attack by a compromised operating system.
        \emph{Effect:} attacks that require control of the operating system are
        removed. Attacks that use a modified copy of the \progname{} app are still
        analyzed in Section~\ref{SecFMEA}. \emph{If false:} an attacker in control
        of the phone could read data stored on it, including the session token.
        Because rewards, balances, and holdings are calculated only on the server
        (Section~\ref{SecSafetyReqs}), such an attacker could affect only that
        phone's own account. The app can also detect rooted or jailbroken phones and
        warn the user, as recommended by the OWASP Mobile Application Security
        Verification Standard (MASVS)~\cite{OWASPMASVS}.
  \item[CA4:] \textbf{Documented platform behaviour.} When the health platform
        and market data provider respond, their application programming interfaces
        behave as their documentation states. \emph{Rationale:} \progname{} depends
        on these services and cannot verify their internal behaviour.
        \emph{Effect:} this does not assume the data they return is accurate,
        complete, or timely; wrong, missing, delayed, and duplicated data are
        analyzed in Section~\ref{SecFMEA}. \emph{If false:} an undocumented change,
        such as a renamed field, appears as failed or invalid responses, which
        \progname{} already handles as missing or wrong data.
  \item[CA5:] \textbf{Hosting security.} The hosting provider protects the
        physical security of its servers and controls its own staff's access to them.
        \emph{Rationale:} these controls are outside the team's reach.
        \emph{Effect:} physical theft from the data centre is removed. Outages, data
        loss, and breaches through \progname{}'s own software or credentials are
        still analyzed in Section~\ref{SecFMEA}. \emph{If false:} because personal
        data is encrypted at rest, including in backups
        (Section~\ref{SecSafetyReqs}), stolen storage would not expose it in
        readable form.
\end{itemize}
