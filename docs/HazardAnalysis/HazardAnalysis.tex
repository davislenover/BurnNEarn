\section{Scope and Purpose of Hazard Analysis} \label{SecScope}

% Instructor prompt (hidden): You should say what loss could be incurred
% because of the hazards.

\progname{} is designed to change how its users behave: to be more active, to
keep up a routine, and to make investing decisions. Features that change
behaviour can also cause harm, even when they work as intended. For example,
one interview participant described a step streak that now makes them go out
walking later than they should (P01). The purpose of this
hazard analysis is to identify how \progname{} could cause harm and to define
requirements that control it. Hazards are identified using Failure Mode and
Effect Analysis (FMEA) in Section~5, and the resulting safety and security
requirements are listed in Section~\ref{SecSafetyReqs} and added to the SRS.
The aim is to reduce each risk as low as reasonably practicable (ALARP).

In-app currency has no real-world value, so \progname{} cannot cause a direct
financial loss. However, given it is expected to simulate real-world trading it can influence a users financial choices outside the app. Table~\ref{TblLosses} lists the losses it can cause. Each FMEA
row and each requirement in Section~\ref{SecSafetyReqs} refers to these
identifiers.

\midrule

The analysis covers the \progname{} mobile app on Android and iOS, its server
and database, and its use of the phone's health platform and the market data
provider. Hazards are considered both when a part fails and when a feature
behaves as intended. The following are out of scope:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The internal workings of the health platforms and the market data
  provider. How their failures appear in \progname{}, such as missing activity
  data or delayed prices, is in scope.
  \item Injuries that are part of a sport or activity itself.
  \item Stretch goals (Problem Statement Section~3). They will be analysed if
  they are built.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Activity Data Integrity}

\hazreq{SAF-7}{Daily earning cap}
{The system shall award a user no more than \texttt{MAX\_DAILY\_CURRENCY}
virtual currency for recorded activity in any calendar day.}
{For a test user whose recorded activity in one calendar day would otherwise
earn more than \texttt{MAX\_DAILY\_CURRENCY}, the balance increases by exactly
\texttt{MAX\_DAILY\_CURRENCY} for that day. Calendar days follow the user's
local time zone.}
{Without a cap, more activity always earns more currency, which rewards
exercising past a healthy amount. One participant admired pushing through
exercise regardless of pain (P02). A cap also limits what non-exercise motion
can earn: phones count steps while riding a bus, and one tracking app was left
running for hours after a ride had ended (P01). The cap applies to currency
rather than to any one activity, so it works the same way for every supported
activity type (Problem Statement Goal 5). The cap limits how much a day can
earn, but it does not judge whether a workout record is genuine.
\reqref{SEC-1} describes this in more detail.
\todo{Choose \texttt{MAX\_DAILY\_CURRENCY} for SRS Appendix C (\#95) once the
earning rate is defined in the SRS.}}

\subsection{Financial Understanding}

\begin{description}

\hazreq{SAF-8}{Simulation notice before the first trade}
{Before the user's first trade, the system shall display a notice stating that
the market is simulated, that virtual currency and holdings have no real-world
value, and that nothing in \progname{} is financial advice. The system shall
not complete the first trade until the user acknowledges the notice.}
{A new test user who attempts their first trade is shown the notice and cannot
complete the trade until they acknowledge it. The notice contains all three
statements: the market is simulated, currency and holdings have no real-world
value, and nothing in \progname{} is financial advice.}
{\progname{} teaches investing by letting users practise, and people learn how
to act from what they practise: one participant said that formal study did not
show them how to act until they had hands-on experience (P02). The lessons a
simulation teaches can carry over to real decisions, and a user could
mistake simulated results or in-app content for advice on what to do with real
money. Users who learn investing on their own also draw on informal sources of
mixed quality: one participant learned from online videos and forums, warned
that it is easy to end up following the wrong kinds of investing content, and
looked for answers tailored to their own situation (P01).}
{FMEA \todo{row ID}, L6 (financial-understanding harm).}
{\todo{SRS section and ID}}
{Capstone.}

\subsection{Social Feed}

\begin{description}

deletes the post or removes one of those friends, the post no longer appears

{Problem Statement Goal 3 measures success by users controlling who sees each

\hazreq{SEC-12}{No location detail in posts}
{The system shall not include location, route or exact time information in
shared posts.}
{No shared post shown to a test user's friends contains a location, a route,
or the exact time an activity took place.}
{A route or location, especially combined with when an activity happened, can

\hazreq{SAF-11}{No rankings between users}
{The system shall not rank users against each other by activity, currency or
portfolio performance.}
{No screen shown to a test user orders users by activity, currency or
portfolio performance, or shows a user's position relative to others.}
{Seeing friends' progress can motivate, but comparison becomes harmful when a
user is far behind (P02). A ranking makes that gap explicit and places someone
last. Leaderboards are a stretch goal (Problem Statement Section~3). If they are
built, they should be opt-in.}
